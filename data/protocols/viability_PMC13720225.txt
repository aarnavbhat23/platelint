## 2. Materials and Methods
2. Materials and Methods 2.1. Chemicals and Reagents The Reg standard (lot: MB5106, purity > 99%) was purchased from Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Selinexor (internal standard, IS; lot: C13J11L117641, purity > 99%) was obtained from Yuanye Bio‐Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HPLC‐grade methanol and acetonitrile were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). HPLC‐grade formic acid was purchased from McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ultrapure water was used throughout the study. Cell fractionation kits for the isolation of cell nucleus and mitochondria were procured from Abbkine Scientific Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). 2.2. Cell Culture The human colorectal cancer cell line HCT116 was a stored cell line maintained in our laboratory. The human colon cancer cell line SW480 was purchased from Xinyu Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HCT116 cells were maintained in McCoy’s 5A medium, and SW480 cells were cultured in Leibovitz’s L‐15 medium; both media were supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. HCT116 cells were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO 2 atmosphere, and SW480 cells were cultured at 37°C without CO 2 . The culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days. Upon reaching 80%–85% confluence, the cells were either passaged or harvested for subsequent experiments. 2.3. LC‐MS/MS Instrumentation Chromatographic analysis was performed using an Agilent 1290 series UHPLC system, equipped with an online degasser, a binary pump, an autosampler, and a thermostated column compartment. The UHPLC system was coupled to an Agilent 6460A triple quadrupole mass spectrometer with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA). Data acquisition and processing were conducted using Agilent MassHunter Workstation Software (Version 6.00). 2.4. Liquid Chromatographic Conditions Separation was achieved using an Agilent Poroshell 120 EC‐C 18 column (3.0 × 100 mm, 2.7 μm) maintained at 35°C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B). Isocratic elution was performed with a mixture of 30% A and 70% B. The total run time was 3.65 min, with a flow rate of 0.45 mL/min. 2.5. Mass Spectrometry Conditions ESI was conducted in positive ionization mode with the capillary voltage set to +4500 V. Data acquisition was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. Nitrogen was employed as the desolvation, sheath, and nebulizing gas. The nebulizer pressure was set to 45 psi, and the desolvation gas was heated to 320°C with a flow rate of 10 L/min. The sheath gas temperature was set to 300°C with a flow rate of 12 L/min. High‐purity nitrogen was used as the collision gas at a pressure of 0.2 MPa. The product ion and fragment structures of Reg and IS are shown in Figure 1 . Preparation of Standard and Quality Control (QC) Solutions Stock solutions of Reg (1000.0, 100.0, and 10.0 μg/mL) were prepared by dissolving 2.0 mg of the standard in methanol. Aliquots of these stock solutions were transferred to 1.5‐mL tubes and stored at −80°C. Working solutions were subsequently prepared by diluting with methanol and were further spiked into blank cell matrix (v:v,1:9) to generate calibration standards at concentrations of 1.0, 2.0, 10.0, 50.0, 200.0, 500.0, 750.0, and 1000.0 ng/mL. QC samples at low, medium, and high concentrations (2.0, 500.0, and 750.0 ng/mL, respectively) were prepared independently in the same manner. The IS stock solution (1.0 mg/mL) was stored at −80°C and diluted with methanol to a concentration of 200.0 ng/mL immediately before use. 2.7. Cell Viability Assay HCT116 and SW480 cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 5 × 10 3 cells per well and cultured overnight in McCoy’s 5A and Leibovitz’s L‐15 medium, respectively. The cells were treated with Reg at concentrations ranging from 0 to 2 μM (0, 0.005, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and 1, 2) in their respective medium for 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a SYNERGY microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, USA). Cell viability was calculated as follows: (OD treated group − OD blank group)/(OD control group − OD blank group) × 100%. 2.8. Cell Sample Collection and Pretreatment HCT116 and SW480 cells were seeded in 6‐well plates at 1 × 10^6 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. Upon reaching 80%–90% confluence, the medium was replaced with fresh McCoy’s 5A or L‐15 medium containing Reg at the IC 50 values, respectively, and the cells were incubated for 12, 24, 36, or 48 h. After incubation, cells were harvested, counted, and washed twice with ice‐cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). Nuclear and mitochondrial fractions were isolated using commercial fractionation kits (ExKineTM Nuclear and Mitochondrial Isolation Kits) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All fractionation procedures were performed using precooled buffers and completed as rapidly as possible under cold conditions to minimize postharvest redistribution of Reg. For whole‐cell samples, cells were extracted with 500 μL of methanol on ice for 2 min, followed by evaporation of the supernatant. The dried residue was resuspended in 200 μL of methanol and centrifuged at 14,500 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was mixed with 300 μL of methanol containing 100 ng/mL IS, vortexed for 2 min, and then centrifuged at 14,500 × g for 10 min. For nuclear and mitochondrial fractions, 100 μL of each extract was mixed with 300 μL of methanol containing the IS (100 ng/mL), vortexed for 2 min, and then centrifuged at 14,500 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to a sample vial for LC‐MS/MS analysis. The measured Reg levels in nuclear and mitochondrial samples should be interpreted as Reg recovered from isolated fractions under the present operational procedure rather than as direct evidence of absolute in situ subcellular localization. All experiments were performed in triplicate. 2.9. Method Validation The developed method was validated in accordance with the bioanalytical guidelines provided by the U.S. FDA and the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition). The following parameters were validated: specificity, linearity, precision, accuracy, matrix effect, recovery, carry‐over, dilution integrity, and stability [ 13 , 14 ]. Specificity was evaluated by analyzing six different lots of blank matrix, blank matrix spiked with the IS, lower limit of quantification(LLOQ) samples, and processed real samples. The method was deemed acceptable if the response of any interfering peak was ≤ 20% of the LLOQ response and ≤ 5% of the IS response. Linearity was assessed by analyzing three independently prepared calibration curves daily over a minimum of 2 days. Calibration curves were constructed using a weighted linear least‐squares regression model by plotting the peak area ratio of analyte to IS against the nominal analyte concentration. TheLLOQ was defined as the lowest concentration on the calibration curve. The back‐calculated concentration of each calibration standard was required to be within ±15% of the nominal value (±20% for the LLOQ). Intraday precision and accuracy were determined by analyzing five replicates of QC samples at three concentration levels (low, medium, high) in a single run. Interday precision and accuracy were assessed by analyzing the same QC levels over a minimum of three separate runs. For both intraday and interday measurements, precision (RSD%) was required to be ≤ 15%, and accuracy(RE%) was required to be within ±15% compared to the nominal value. Extraction recovery was determined by comparing the peak areas of analytes spiked into the matrix before extraction with those spiked into the blank matrix extract postextraction at the same concentration. The matrix effect was evaluated by comparing the peak areas of post‐extraction spiked samples with those of solvent‐substituted standard solutions at equivalent concentrations. RSD% of the matrix effect and extraction recovery factors ≤ 15% were considered acceptable, indicating consistent ion suppression or enhancement from the matrix. Carry‐over was assessed by injecting a blank sample immediately following the injection of an upper limit of quantification (ULOQ) standard. The response of the analyte in the blank sample was required to be ≤ 20% of the LLOQ and ≤ 5% of the IS. Dilution integrity was evaluated by diluting samples with concentrations above the ULOQ with blank matrix into the calibration range. Five replicates per dilution factor were analyzed, and the precision was required to be ≤ 15% and accuracy to be within ±15%. Stability was investigated using QC samples at low, medium, and high concentrations under the following conditions: bench‐top stability at room temperature for 4 h, processed sample stability in the autosampler (0, 12, and 24 h), three freeze–thaw cycles, and long‐term storage at −80°C for 1 month. Stability was confirmed if the measured concentration deviated not more than ±15% from the nominal concentration. 2.10. Data Analysis All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Intergroup comparisons were performed using an unpaired Student’s t ‐test or a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To explore the potential relationship between intracellular Reg exposure and cellular response, the cell viability data and intracellular exposure data were integrated descriptively. Because the cell viability assay was performed as a 48‐h concentration–response experiment, whereas intracellular and fraction‐associated Reg exposure was measured over time at the corresponding IC50 concentrations, the two datasets were not fully matched for formal exposure–response modeling. Therefore, the integrated analysis was considered exploratory.
