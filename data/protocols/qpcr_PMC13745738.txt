## Step-by-step method details
Step-by-step method details The following steps describe the representative experimental workflow used to generate input material for CleanFinder analysis. These steps are included to support reproducibility of the associated study but are not required for users who already have suitable amplicon sequencing FASTQ files. The representative genome engineering workflow is illustrated in Figure 2 . Note: Users who already have demultiplexed FASTQ files can skip the wet-lab steps (steps 1–29) and proceed directly to CleanFinder upload and analysis (step 30). Users starting from edited cell material can proceed to amplicon sequencing (step 18). Figure 2 Genome engineering workflow for generating edited cell samples Schematic representation of the experimental steps preceding CleanFinder analysis. Users design the editing strategy, prepare nuclease, editor, guide RNA, pegRNA, or donor reagents, deliver the reagents to the selected cell type, recover or enrich edited cells, and extract genomic DNA for downstream amplicon sequencing. Design and cloning of targeting reagents Timing: 3–5 days 1. Design guide RNAs (gRNAs) targeting the genomic region of interest using established prediction tools, such as CHOPCHOP, 4 CRISPOR, 7 or equivalent platforms. a. Select gRNAs based on predicted on-target activity and minimal off-target potential. b. For PE experiments, design PE guide RNAs (pegRNAs) using established computational predictions tools such as PE-Designer. 5 Note: We recommend selecting pegRNAs with a primer binding site (PBS) length of approximately 12–14 bp and a reverse transcription template (RTT) length of around 16–18 bp to support efficient and precise editing. 2. Introduce the selected gRNA(s) into target cells using an appropriate genome editing delivery strategy. In this protocol, gRNAs were cloned into the pSpCas9(BB)-2A-GFP (PX458) expression vector as described by Ran et al., 8 and plasmid constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing prior to use. Alternatively, preassembled Cas9–gRNA ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes may be delivered. Note: When plasmid-based delivery methods are used, clonal cell lines should be screened for potential vector integration events. We recommend performing PCR-based analysis for plasmid backbone sequences following clone isolation to confirm the absence of random genomic integration. 3. For KI experiments, design a single-stranded donor oligonucleotide (ssODN) containing the desired sequence modification and flanked by homology arms. For efficient homology-directed repair (HDR), we recommend homology arms of approximately 30–40 nucleotides on both sides of the intended edit. a. Whenever possible, introduce a silent mutation within the PAM, preferably at a critical PAM nucleotide, to prevent re-cleavage of the edited allele following successful genome editing. b. If modification of the PAM sequence is not feasible, introduce one or more silent mutations within the gRNA recognition sequence to reduce Cas nuclease binding and cleavage of the edited allele. c. Order the donor oligonucleotide from a commercial supplier. In this study, Alt-R™ HDR donor oligonucleotides (Integrated DNA Technologies, IDT) were used. Note: Donor oligonucleotides should be designed to avoid introducing unintended amino acid changes, cryptic splice sites, or regulatory sequence alterations whenever possible. Oligos can be designed using the CleanFinder module named Oligo (OligoDonor Designer). 4. For PE experiments, clone pegRNAs into pU6-tevopreq1-GG-acceptor as described by Doman et al. 9 Delivery of genome-editing reagents Timing: 1–3 days 5. Prepare the cells of interest for genome editing according to established protocols for the selected cell type. Ensure that cells are healthy, actively proliferating, and at an appropriate density for the chosen delivery method. 6. Deliver the genome editing reagents using an appropriate strategy for the selected cell type and editing modality. a. For nuclease-mediated editing, deliver the nuclease and guide RNA as plasmid DNA, mRNA, ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes, viral vectors, or equivalent formats. Select the appropriate reagents for your cell type. b. For KI experiments, co-deliver a suitable donor template (e.g., ssODN, dsDNA donor, plasmid donor, or viral donor template) when required. c. Where appropriate, e.g., for KI experiments, use reagents that enhance homology-directed repair (HDR) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations (e.g., for hiPSCs Alt-R™ HDR enhancer (1:1000)). Note: For transfection, medium should be free of antibiotics. Chose the delivery method, e.g., lipofection, electroporation, nucleofection, or viral vectors, depending on your target cell type. ssODNs may be toxic to cells. Avoid using more than 1 μg per well of a 6-well plate. Option A : Plasmid-based delivery for knockout, knock-in and prime editing (example workflow used in this study). d. Plate the number of cells suitable for your target cell type. For hiPSCs, plate at-least 3 × 10 5 cells per well of a 6-well plate in complete growth medium containing CEPT. e. After 24 h, aspirate the medium to remove dead cells and debris. Replenish with fresh growth medium containing CEPT. f. Introduce the nuclease and guide RNA expression constructs into cells using an appropriate transfection reagent or electroporation system. i. For KI experiments, co-deliver the donor template together with the editing construct, e.g., combine 2 μg PX458 plasmid containing sgRNA and 1 μg of Alt-RTM HDR donor oligo. ii. For PE, pipet 0.5 μg of pegRNA plasmid pU6-tevopreq1-GG-acceptor and 1.5 μg of pCMV-PEmax-P2A-GFP in Opti-MEM. g. In another reaction tube, pipette the appropriate volume of lipofectamine stem transfection reagent in of Opti-MEM. We recommend using lipofectamine reagent to plasmid ratio (3:1) or according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. h. Combine the contents of both tubes and incubate the mixture for 15 min at 15–25 °C. After incubation, add the transfection mix to the cells dropwise while gently swirling the plate to ensure even distribution. i. After 24 h, replace the medium with growth medium. Observe the cells under a microscope for cell death and fluorescence. Proceed to FACS, MACS or antibiotic selection depending upon the selection marker. We routinely use FACS (described below). Option B : Ribonucleoprotein (RNP)-based delivery. j. Assemble guide RNAs with recombinant nuclease protein according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. i. In this example, we resuspend Alt-R™ crRNA and tracrRNA in IDT duplex buffer to a final concentration of 100 μM. ii. In a clean PCR tube, combine crRNA and tracrRNA to generate a sgRNA at a final concentration of 50 μM. iii. Heat the mixture to 95 °C for 5 min and cool down to 15–25 °C at the bench. iv. Prepare the RNP mix as described in the materials and equipment section and incubate for 20 min at 15–25 °C. Note: Cas9 is supplied in glycerol-containing buffer, and may be viscous. k. For KI experiments, add 1 μg of the Alt-R™ HDR donor oligo to the preassembled RNP and mix gently. Note: ssODNs may be toxic to cells. Avoid using more than 1 μg per well of a 6-well plate. l. Deliver the RNP complex using a suitable electroporation or nucleofection platform optimized for the selected cell type. Here, we used the Neon™ NxT electroporation system with 10 μl tips. i. Generate a single-cell suspension of the KOLF2.1J cells: wash once with PBS, add 500 μl Accutase, incubate at 37 °C for 10 min, harvest the cells using PBS, centrifuge at 300 × g for 5 min, and resuspend the cells in electroporation buffer. ii. Transfer RNP-cell mixture to the tip, and electroporate at 1,300 V, 10 ms, and a single pulse. m. Allow cells to recover under standard culture conditions before proceeding with downstream selection or genotyping. For hiPSCs, we immediately resuspend cells in growth medium containing CEPT, and transfer them to Geltrex-coated wells of a 6-well plate. 7. Enrich or select edited cells if desired. a. Fluorescent reporters may be used for enrichment by FACS (e.g., fluorescent Cas9 or tracrRNA). b. Magnetic enrichment (MACS) may be used when compatible markers are available. 10 c. Antibiotic selection may be applied when resistance markers are incorporated into the editing construct. d. Alternative enrichment strategies may be used according to the experimental design. 8. Verify successful reagent delivery using an appropriate readout, such as fluorescence reporter expression, antibiotic resistance, molecular genotyping, or equivalent methods. CRITICAL: GE efficiency depends strongly on cell health, reagent quality, and delivery conditions. Human iPSCs should actively proliferate, form compact, multi-layered colonies with defined edges and a high nucleus to cytoplasm ratio, show no signs of contamination and less than 5 % spontaneous differentiation. For delivery, antibiotic-free medium should be used during transfection. Preferably, RNPs should be used over plasmids for increased efficiency of editing hiPSCs. Optimization of the delivery method for the cell type of interest is often required before large-scale editing experiments are initiated. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting Timing: 2–3 h This section describes how to enrich transfected cells by fluorescence-activated cell sorting and, if required, recover bulk-edited populations or isolate single-cell-derived clones for downstream genotyping. CRITICAL: Always include antibiotics during cell sorting to prevent contamination. 9. Prepare the cells for FACS 24 h post transfection. A reduced percentage of GFP positive cells are typically observed, if FACS is performed at later timepoints, i.e., after 48–72hrs. 10. Prepare fresh FACS buffer (PBS without magnesium and calcium supplemented with 2 % FCS and CEPT; sterile-filtered (0.2 μM, Sarstedt)). 11. Obtain single cell solutions as described above. 12. Mix the cell suspension thoroughly and pass through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove cell aggregates and obtain a single cell suspension. 13. Centrifuge at 300 × g for 5 min and resuspend the cell pellet with 300 μl of FACS buffer. 14. Sort the GFP-positive cells and collect the cells in growth medium containing CEPT. 15. Centrifuge at 300 × g for 5 min and resuspend the cell pellet in 2 mL of growth medium containing CEPT and plate the cells into a well of a 6-well plate. 16. After 24 h exchange medium to growth medium, and allow the FACS-sorted bulk cells grow until they are 60–80 % confluent. 17. Proceed to bulk analysis or/and isolate single clones as described below: a. Prepare a single cell suspension as described above, and pass through 70 μm cell strainer to remove aggregates. Alternatively, single-sort cells into wells of a 96-well plate during enrichment of transfected cells (steps 12–15 above). b. Seed cells into Geltrex-coated wells of a 96-well plate at a calculated density of 1.5 cells per well in 50 μl of growth medium containing CEPT, and incubate at 37°C. c. Starting after 48 h post plating, exchange growth medium every other day with fresh growth medium until visible colony formation (usually at least 2 weeks). d. Once colonies reach 60% confluency, prepare lysis (for genotyping) and maintenance (for expansion) plates by equally splitting the colonies into separate 96 well plates. Amplicon sequencing Timing: 2–3 days This section describes how to lyse edited cells, amplify the target locus, add sample-specific barcodes and sequencing adapters, and generate FASTQ files for CleanFinder analysis. The amplicon sequencing workflow is illustrated in Figure 3 . 18. Design primers for targeted amplicon sequencing that flank the genome-edited region and generate an amplicon suitable for the chosen sequencing platform. Ensure that the expected amplicon spans the editing site and is compatible with downstream library preparation and sequencing requirements. Note: Primer design can be performed using standard primer design software or online tools, such as BrowserGenome 11 ( https://www.browsergenome.org/ ). 19. Prepare DNA lysis buffer consisting of PBS supplemented with 1% (v/v) Tween 20 and Proteinase K (20 mg/mL stock) diluted to a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL. Add 50 μL of lysis buffer to each well of a 96-well plate containing cell pellets and incubate at 56 °C for 1 h. 20. Following lysis, incubate the plate at 95°C for 15 min to inactivate Proteinase K. Note: Incomplete inactivation of Proteinase K may inhibit downstream PCR amplification. 21. For the first PCR step of Illumina library preparation, assemble the targeted amplification reaction using the first PCR mix given in the materials and equipment section. Note: For ONT, refer to the library preparation steps of the Native Barcoding Kit 96 V14 (SQK-NBD114.96): Ligation sequencing amplicons – Native Barcoding Kit 96 V14 . 22. Users design primers flanking the edited region, amplify the target locus, add sample barcodes or indexes, perform library quality control and quantification, and sequence the libraries using Illumina, ONT, PacBio, or another FASTQ-generating sequencing platform. 23. Incorporate sample-specific barcodes and sequencing adapters into the amplicons to enable multiplexed sequencing. In this example, barcodes were introduced by a second-round PCR (index PCR) using indexed primers and the index PCR mix given in the materials and equipment section. Alternatively, use a commercial library preparation kit that incorporates sample barcodes and sequencing adapters according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Optional: Check PCR products on a 1.5 % agarose gel to confirm size and specificity. 24. Perform the PCR using the same conditions used for the first PCR described before. 25. Pool the indexed PCR products and assess library quality by agarose gel electrophoresis. For high-throughput experiments, individual reactions may be pooled prior to purification. 26. Using a clean scalpel, excise the DNA fragment corresponding to the expected library size from the agarose gel. In our workflow, the final indexed amplicon library was approximately 450 bp in length. 27. Purify the DNA using a gel extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 28. Quantify the purified library using a fluorometric assay (e.g., Qubit 4 Fluorometer) or a qPCR-based library quantification method (e.g., the NEBNext Library Quant Kit for Illumina). qPCR-based quantification is generally recommended for accurate estimation of sequenceable library molecules prior to sequencing. Prepare and load the library according to the recommendations of the selected sequencing platform and library preparation workflow. Note: Amplicon sequencing libraries are typically low-diversity libraries due to the high sequence similarity among reads. In this study, libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq i100 Plus instrument, which demonstrated robust performance with low-diversity amplicon libraries. Library loading concentrations and sequencing parameters should be optimized according to the manufacturer's current recommendations for the instrument and chemistry used (the optimal value depends on the clustering behaviour of the instrument, and we recommend loading between 60 and 80 pM of the final library as a starting point; see Illumina's loading concentration recommendations for the MiSeq i100 Series ). 29. Once the run is complete, extract the FASTQ files according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and proceed with downstream analysis genome editing using CleanFinder. Upload data and analysis Timing: 5–60 min This section describes how to define the analysis window, provide the reference sequence, upload FASTQ files, configure the appropriate analysis mode, and start CleanFinder processing. The CleanFinder analysis and result interpretation workflow is summarized in Figure 4 . Note: This step includes selecting the files for analysis and setting the parameters. Timing depends on the number and size of files uploaded, and the local computational power. 30. Specify the window of data analysis. a. Provide reference DNA sequence and specify anchors. i. Manual: paste reference sequence manually. ii. Ensembl: retrieve the reference sequence from the database. Select the species of the amplicon of interest, provide the gene symbol, enter the gRNA sequence used to position the analysis anchors, and click Fetch to obtain a centered reference sequence. Note: In references retrieved from Ensembl, exonic sequences are shown in capital letters and intronic sequences in lowercase letters, so exon and intron boundaries can be read directly from the retrieved reference. Optional: KI oligo: specify knock-in sequence for targeted GE experiments b. 5′ and 3′ padding: specify sequence length up- and downstream of the provided gRNA which is ignored for the actual analysis window. 31. Prepare the sequencing data for upload. Note: CleanFinder has been validated with data in FASTQ format from long-read sequencers (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, PacBio) and short-read sequencers (Illumina), and is expected to work with amplicon sequencing data in FASTQ format from any other NGS method. a. Select files for upload by drag & drop or browse (“Sequencing Reads”). 32. Set parameters for sequencing data analysis (“Configuration”). a. Select “Analysis Mode” depending on GE experiment setup. This can be either one of: i. “Standard (Cas9)” (default) for CRISPR/Cas9-based and other double-strand-break nuclease KO experiments (e.g., TALENs, ZFNs). ii. “Standard (Cas12/Cpf1)” for CRISPR/Cas12-based KO experiments. iii. “Prime Editing” for resolving complex mixtures of PE outcomes. iv. “Base Editing” for resolving single nucleotide substitutions introduced by BE. Note: Although the modes are named for the most common systems (Cas9, Cas12/Cpf1, prime editing, and base editing), CleanFinder is editor-agnostic and can resolve essentially any genome-engineering outcome. We have, for example, applied it to mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) BE. For non-standard editors, select the closest mode and adapt the alignment scoring matrix under Advanced Alignment Parameters. b. Specify “Advanced Alignment Parameters” to fine-tune analysis: i. Set settings for the allowed number of mismatches for anchor search. ii. Adjust the alignment scoring matrix: match score, mismatch penalty, gap-opening penalty, and gap-extension penalty. Tuning these values tailors the alignment to the expected edit type — higher gap penalties suppress spurious indels when scoring substitutions (e.g., nuclear or mtDNA BE), whereas lower gap penalties favour the detection of larger insertions and deletions. iii. Activate a speed-boosting mode for faster analysis. Two platform-specific k-mer-seeded options are available, ONT Fast (K-mer Seeded) and Illumina Fast (K-mer Seeded). Each derives a short seed from the 5′ anchor (a 13-mer for ONT and a 10-mer for Illumina/standard) and rapidly skips any read that does not contain this seed on either strand, so that full alignment is performed only on candidate on-target reads. This greatly increases throughput, but reads carrying sequencing errors within the seed region (more common in noisy ONT data) may be skipped, which can lower sensitivity; leave these options off when maximal sensitivity or precision is required. iv. Set the pre-analysis read quality filter by specifying a minimum per-read PHRED score (default: 15). Reads whose mean per-base PHRED quality falls below this threshold are discarded before alignment; as a guideline, use approximately 25 or higher for Illumina and PacBio data and approximately 15 or lower for ONT data. 33. Analyze results Timing: 5–15 min This section describes how to inspect CleanFinder outputs, including aligned-read metrics, edit distributions, genotype-level calls, repair-pathway annotations, clone summaries, and downloadable reports. Note: Timing depends on the user, but obtaining comprehensive results is fast. CleanFinder offers extensive comprehensive analysis and visualization of GE outcomes. 34. “File Overview” provides an overview of the GE outcomes of analyzed files in either grid or heatmap view, both aggregated as well as by sample. 35. “Edit Distribution” provides detailed distribution of identified genomic edits either grouped in detail by base changes (“indels”) or by the type of edit (“category”). 36. Information on total number of reads aligned after filtering, editing efficiency in %, percentage of out-of-frame mutations, in-frame mutations, and precise editing (PE or KI) are provided. 37. “Genotype Distribution” provides detailed per sample information on the type of GE outcomes identified, the exact aligned sequence, and RIMA-based analysis 6 of the type of repair pathway involved in repairing a DNA double-strand break. This analysis can be filtered for the minimum number of reads for an outcome to be taken into account, the minimal relative occurrence in the sample, and the minimal PHRED score to be taken into account. Note: Adjusting these parameters does not require additional computational power. 38. Below the detailed genotype information, graphical visualizations on indel distribution, frameshift distribution, microhomology length, repair pathway, and PAM proximity of deletions, are provided. Note: For each genotype, CleanFinder displays a set of badges summarizing the edit and its likely repair outcome. The edit-type badge labels the read as wild-type (WT), substitution (SNP), insertion (INS), deletion (DEL), knock-in / homology-directed repair (HDR/KI; read matches the intended knock-in template), base edit (BE; intended base conversion without a length change), or prime edit (PE; read matches the intended prime-edit sequence). A frame badge marks indels as In-Frame when the net length change is a multiple of three and Out-of-Frame (frameshift) otherwise; wild-type and substitution-only reads carry no frame badge. For deletions, a repair-pathway badge is assigned from the microhomology detected at the deletion junction: non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) when junction microhomology is absent or shorter than 2 bp, and microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ; also termed MHEJ or alternative end joining) when the junction carries microhomology of at least 2 bp. The length of the microhomology tract is reported as a microhomology (MH) badge (e.g., MH-1, MH-2, MH-3, and longer), and the position of each deletion relative to the cut site and PAM is shown. This Rational Indel Meta-Analysis (RIMA v2)-based annotation, 6 together with the REPAIRome framework 12 (RIMA v2 available at https://github.com/Ghahfarokhi/RIMA ), distinguishes precise from error-prone repair and supports interpretation of editing fidelity and reproducibility. 39. Below, a concise summary of the individual clones is provided. Downloads of a HTML report, a spreadsheet summary, and of the visualizations, can be performed from this tab. Note: In the clone summary, a clone is flagged as a KO only when it is out-of-frame and two distinct edited sequences are detected (a biallelic, out-of-frame genotype). To avoid mistaking allelic dropout for a homozygous KO in diploid cells, a single out-of-frame sequence is not called a KO. The two alleles are expected within an approximately 70:30 ratio; if one allele falls below ∼30% of the reads, the clone is not flagged as KO. Rapid screening with Turbo Mode Timing: 2–15 min for a 96-well plate This section describes how to use Turbo Mode for rapid primary screening of plate-scale FASTQ datasets before reanalyzing selected samples with the standard browser workflow or Python CLI. Note: Turbo Mode (TurboMode.html) is a lightweight, high-throughput alternative to the standard analysis, optimized for the rapid screening of large experiments, e.g., 96- or 384-well plates. Whereas the standard browser application and the Python CLI align every read with a rigorous glocal (anchor-guided, constrained semi-global) algorithm, Turbo Mode uses a faster heuristic alignment. This increases throughput at the cost of some precision when calling complex or non-standard variants. 40. Open TurboMode.html, paste the WT reference sequence and, optionally, the gRNA sequence (used for highlighting). 41. Name the FASTQ files by well position for automatic plate assignment: the pattern is a letter A–H followed by a number 1–12 at the start of the filename (e.g., A1_VHL.fastq, B3-sample.fq, C12.fastq). Files without a recognizable well position are assigned sequentially (A1, A2, …, H12). 42. Upload one or more FASTQ files (or paste sequences directly, one per line), then click Run Analysis. 43. Inspect the plate view. Results are displayed as a 96-well grid (A1–H12) in which each well is represented by a pie chart of the allele distribution, colour-coded as wild-type (green), HDR (purple), in-frame indel (blue), out-of-frame indel (red), and substitution/SNP (amber). 44. Wells with fewer than 100 aligned reads are shown dimmed (reduced opacity, dashed border) to flag data insufficient for reliable genotyping; hover over a well to see its exact read count. 45. Click any well to view its detailed allele breakdown below the plate, including the allele table (genotype, alignment, type, size delta, and read count) and an indel-size distribution chart. Use the Min Reads filter (default 100) to hide low-frequency alleles. CRITICAL: Turbo Mode is intended for rapid categorization and primary screening, not for the detailed reconstruction of complex mixtures. Reanalyze prioritized or ambiguous samples with the standard browser application or the Python CLI, which use the full glocal alignment. Inspect the outputs. The optional PDF adds a colour-coded summary dashboard and detailed alignment blocks for the most abundant alleles. Note: The --top and --show-all flags affect only the console and PDF display. The results.csv always contains every detected genotype regardless of these flags. Allelic dropout analysis Timing: 5–20 min per sample This section describes how to use long-read amplicon data and heterozygous SNVs/SNPs to assess whether an apparently homozygous editing outcome reflects true biallelic editing or loss of one allele. The Allelic Dropout analyzer detects heterozygous SNVs/SNPs in long-read amplicon data to determine whether an apparently homozygous editing outcome reflects true biallelic editing or the loss (“dropout”) of one allele, for example through a large deletion or rearrangement that would otherwise be misread as a homozygous knockout. Open the Allelic Dropout in any modern browser; no installation or server is required. 56. Open the Allelic Dropout module and upload the sequencing file (FASTQ). The module is optimized for long-read (ONT or PacBio) data; ONT super-accuracy basecalling is recommended. 57. Define a stable anchor sequence of approximately 15–20 bp. It must be unique, present in all reads, and located in a conserved region away from the expected editing site and candidate SNV/SNP (we recommend an anchor at least 100–200 bp from the expected edit and any candidate variant). 58. Provide the wild-type reference by pasting the amplicon sequence or using the Fetch from Gene Symbol tool. Supplying a gRNA during fetch centers the reference on the region of interest. 59. Set the parameters: scan window (bases searched on either side of the anchor; default 500 bp), minimum coverage (reads required to call a SNV/SNP; default 500), and the minimum/maximum allele frequency expected for a heterozygous SNV/SNP (default 30–70%). 60. Click “Run Analysis”. The tool aligns reads around the stable anchor, builds a base-frequency matrix for every position in the scan window, and identifies high-coverage positions that show two distinct bases within the target frequency range. 61. Read the call. BIALLELIC (green) indicates a high-confidence heterozygous SNV/SNP, indicating both alleles are present. MONOALLELIC (amber) indicates that no heterozygous SNV/SNP was found, which may reflect allelic dropout or a naturally homozygous sample. ERROR (red) indicates that the analysis failed, typically due to poor anchor matching or insufficient coverage. 62. Review the SNV/SNP table: the position relative to the anchor, the identity and frequency of the two observed alleles, and a visual snippet of the reference context around each SNP. CRITICAL: Place the anchor outside the edited region. An anchor that overlaps the edit can fail to match dropout-affected reads and bias the call. Note: A monoallelic call alone is not sufficient to prove an allelic dropout event. Where parental or unedited control data are available, confirm against known heterozygous SNVs/SNPs in the amplicon before concluding that an allele has been lost. Genome Viewer Timing: 5–15 min This section describes how to visualize gene structure, locate guide RNAs, predict nuclease cut sites, and preview the coding- or protein-level consequences of candidate indels. The Genome Viewer module is a standalone, browser-based companion tool for visualizing a gene, locating a gRNA on the transcript, predicting Cas9 and Cas12a cut sites, and simulating the protein-level impact of a CRISPR indel—all without running the command-line pipeline. It is particularly useful when planning functional gene knockouts. Open genome_viewer.html directly in your browser; it fetches gene structure and sequence data live from Ensembl and UCSC. 63. Open genome viewer module and enter the HGNC gene symbol (e.g., VHL, BRCA1, SNAI1 ) and, optionally, the protospacer (gRNA) sequence without PAM. 64. Click Draw structure to render the canonical RefSeq transcript as an exon–intron diagram. Use the zoom, Fit to View, and Labels controls; the mouse wheel zooms, and Shift+wheel or click-drag pans the diagram. 65. Optionally click View in UCSC to open the locus in an embedded UCSC Genome Browser view (hg38). 66. With a gRNA entered, the viewer overlays the protospacer span (amber), the Cas9 blunt double-strand break 3 bp upstream of the NGG PAM (red dashed line), and the Cas12a staggered cut zone 18–23 nt from the 5′-TTTV PAM-proximal end (orange shading). An info panel reports the exact genomic coordinates of the gRNA and both cut sites and whether the gRNA was found on the forward or reverse strand. Note: If the gRNA spans an exon–intron boundary it may not be found in the genomic window; in that case enter the CDS cut position manually in the mutation-impact section. 67. Click Show CDS & Protein and select a transcript (canonical is recommended) to display the full coding sequence and translated protein and to auto-detect the cut position. 68. In the Mutation Impact Preview, choose the nuclease (Cas9 or Cas12a), click Auto-detect from gRNA to fill the CDS cut position, select an indel size using the presets (−1, −2, −4, −5, +1, +3) or a custom value, and click Simulate. 69. Read the simulation result: the frame call (In-Frame or Out-of-Frame/frameshift); a nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) badge predicting whether a premature stop codon is NMD-sensitive or NMD-escaped under the 50–55 nt exon-junction-complex rule 13 ; summary metrics (indel size, first affected codon, new stop codon position, and percentage of wild-type protein retained); and a side-by-side, color-coded alignment of the wild-type and mutant protein sequences. Note: The NMD prediction approximates the 50–55 nt rule using an estimated last exon-junction position. For a definitive NMD assessment, validate with experimental data or a dedicated splice-aware tool. Companion sequence-design tools (optional) Timing: 5–10 min CleanFinder includes two companion utilities that support experiment design but are not required for genotyping analysis. The OligoDonor Designer module aids the design of single-stranded HDR donor oligonucleotides for knock-in experiments: it loads the target transcript, locates the gRNA, lets the user define the intended protein-level edit, and proposes a donor with homology arms and silent mutations that prevent re-cleavage of the edited allele, while reporting the selected strand and melting temperature. The Reverse Complement module provides quick sequence operations, including reverse, complement, reverse-complement, and DNA/RNA conversion, together with basic primer-design helpers and a codon reference table. Both tools are convenient for preparing reagents and reference sequences but play no role in the CleanFinder analysis itself.
